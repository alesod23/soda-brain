In thesis text (and any academic text for him) never write a claim as the output of a search ("the searches surfaced", "found in the searches of Appendix E", "the legal sources read for this thesis"). Name the main places where a topic is treated, concretely (for §5 ArbnErfG: commentary, BGH case law, DPMA arbitration board, economics of employee inventions), and say of each what it does and what it lacks. The appendix with the search is mentioned once, briefly, for details.

Every cited work gets a real summary sentence: what it argues, on what basis ("underperforms in what way? based on what?"), never a three-word label. A list of literature has to show why these works were chosen (one per question of the field, or similar), so it does not look like random papers.

**Why:** his words on 2026-09-27: "Everything sounds so AI, like it's the result of a search"; "you're opening something and then you're not saying it"; "Are they MECE or close to being MECE?". Hedged search-language reads as machine text to his supervisor.

**How to apply:** write assertively about what was read, keep the honesty in the comment reply or changelog (what was not opened, what rests on genre knowledge), not in the thesis sentence. When he gives feedback on a tracked change, also add a Word comment "Comment from Alessandro (date): ..." in that thread summarising it, so he sees the history when he reviews again. Word gotcha: edits through COM fail while a comment card is selected in his window; ask him to click in the body text.
